# How to research a UK grime artist

Start with a person, a place or a surviving item. Finish with something another person can check and, where the evidence gives you a name, a route to the next artist. An unsigned producer credited on one upload deserves the same care as an MC with a dozen interviews.

## Choose a starting point

| If you have… | Look here first | What to take forward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A stage name and a town | An artist interview, a local promoter’s interview, community radio, a venue programme or local press. Search the name in quotes alongside the town, crew, station or an era. | The exact wording about place, role or period. Read past the biography to questions about early crews, producers, peers and mentors. |
| A crew, station, night or nearly forgotten channel | Start from the organiser, station or original uploader. Try Rinse, JDZmedia, SBTV, Too Hard For TV, label Bandcamp pages and collectors’ set indexes as appropriate to the place and era. | Open the actual episode or item. Check descriptions, track credits, sleeve notes and introductions. Record the name, role and a timestamp if you heard it. The Grime Archive is a route into set listings. [The Grime Archive](https://www.grimearchive.org/about) |
| A missing page, Myspace handle or deleted video | Look for a contemporary blog embed, label announcement, event listing, forum link or flyer that printed the URL. Extract the profile path, numeric ID or video ID. | Search the exact address in Wayback. Keep the item that supplied it. An embed and the upload it points to share a source. |
| A place with few search results | Start again from a local studio, youth music project, pirate station, community archive or club night. Search a year and venue together. Check local papers, listings pages, flyers and tape inlays. | A date and a promoter can locate a physical listing. In Derby, start with the city’s Local Studies collection. Elsewhere use the equivalent local service. A library catalogue or shelfmark tells a volunteer what to request. [Derby Local Studies](https://www.inderby.org.uk/libraries/whats-on-offer/local-studies-collections/) · [British Library Sound and Vision](https://www.bl.uk/collection/sound-and-vision) |
| Someone who remembers the scene | A willing participant or collector can identify a spelling, crew or earlier source. Ask what they personally saw, who was credited, how they knew them and what item might survive. | Record the date, the speaker’s connection, agreed attribution and exactly what may be shared. A human organiser handles contact and permissions privately. [Oral History Society guidance](https://ohs.org.uk/for-beginners/) · [Informed consent](https://ohs.org.uk/legal-and-ethical-advice/informed-consent/) |

Use a fan list, Discogs, MusicBrainz, a forum or a newspaper recap to find the underlying source or a new spelling. Credit the person who surfaced it. Decide what a source establishes from its author, date, purpose and wording. A small channel’s original credit may answer a question that a national paper never asked.

## Follow the people

Plan a search around where people left traces in that particular place and era. Keep a list of source families already tried and choose an independent one for the next pass. Examples include:

* **Radio and sound.** Check pirate and community schedules, host pages, cassette labels, Mixcloud show notes, SoundCloud descriptions, broadcast archives and spoken introductions. Capture the station, show, date and exact credit or timecode. [NTS archive](https://www.nts.live/explore) · [Mixcloud](https://www.mixcloud.com/)
* **Local media and the earlier web.** Look through crew sites, forums, fanzines, blogs and blogrolls, old social profiles, uploader channels and linked comments. A contemporary embed or print URL can connect a vanished page to a stable identifier. Use Wayback when there is an address to recover. Treat user comments as leads and verify them against the item or a participant.
* **Physical and community collections.** Look for youth-club programmes, event flyers, open-mic listings, record-shop notices, MC clash DVDs, tape packs, CD-R sleeves, small-press listings and collectors’ notes. Record the item, holder’s agreed public reference, side or page, date and spelling. A warm-up slot printed in small type can be the only route to a name.
* **People who were there.** Include fans, engineers, promoters, DJs and local organisers as sources. Ask who was on before the headliner, who made the beat, which names changed, and what can be shared. Preserve a lead when only memory survives. Give locality, role and identity their own evidence.

For a geographical survey, keep one entry per **place, period and collection searched**. Note how far through the collection you got and which roles or nearby towns remain uncovered. Start some passes from an unsearched collection even when established artists still have open branches. Track named people, unresolved credits and collections examined. An artist can qualify through one reviewed trace of participation even with no known release. Once the artist is identifiable, spend the next discovery pass looking for someone missing.

1. **Check what is already known.** Search existing artists, crews, aliases, unresolved names, previous searches and live tasks. For a common name, combine it with a station, crew, producer, era or original channel. Record the spelling exactly before trying variants.
2. **Read the evidence in context.** Who is speaking? Who is being named, in what capacity and when? “Eyez recommended Dubzy”, “these names share a billing” and “this handle is credited for production” describe different relationships. A crew has an entry of its own. Keep ambiguous handles unresolved until evidence connects them to a person.
3. **Follow one promising name into another source.** Search the newly found spelling with the shared context, then open an original upload, interview, programme or credit. Prefer a small or overlooked name when several routes are equally well supported. Record other useful names as leads, with the precise paragraph, track or timestamp that produced them. Save a reference to the item once so several researchers can use it.
4. **Check geography separately.** Distinguish grew up in, worked in, represented a city, guested on a show and played a venue. A Derby MC’s producer could be based anywhere. A crew can span cities. Record the supported city or town and the period and relationship the evidence establishes. Omit neighbourhood attributions from public profiles.
5. **Hand off one answerable question.** Give the parent artist or crew, the newly found spelling, exact relationship, source, locator and what evidence would settle the next question. Check the queue before opening it. If it exists, add the new citation. If it loops back to the starting artist, link the records and move to a fresh question. When the trail runs out, log the actual queries, items tried and what would justify another attempt. The editor can accept an honest dead end.

Periodically start from a different decade, neighbourhood, role or collection. Following one prominent MC’s circle will favour the people that MC knew. An earlier crew’s sleeve or local fanzine offers a genuinely different starting point.

## Work a Wayback lead

1. Get the original address from an actual item. Keep the URL as printed and the stable identifier, including YouTube’s `v` parameter. A Myspace artist name alone leaves the profile URL uncertain. Search old inbound links for a handle, profile path or numeric `friendID`.
2. Enter the address in the Wayback calendar around the relevant year. Try observed address variants, including `www`, old hosts or embed/watch forms with the same video ID. Note which form and time window you tried. For an agent, the Internet Archive’s Availability API finds the closest indexed capture. The CDX index can list captures within a chosen date range. [Wayback guide](https://help.archive.org/help/using-the-wayback-machine/) · [Internet Archive API](https://archive.org/help/wayback_api.php)
3. Open the returned capture. Inspect the title, channel or account, description and relevant passage. Read the timestamp of the page you actually reached. Following a link can change the capture date or take you to the live web. A successful HTTP response can contain a sign-in screen, generic player or redirect.
4. Record the original URL, full timestamped replay URL, capture date, page or upload date if shown, and date checked. Say separately whether the text was readable and whether the media was played. Credit a spoken identification only after listening to that timestamp. A preserved watch page can establish a printed credit even when playback is unknown.
5. Try a nearby capture or a contemporary page that linked to the item. If nothing suitable turns up, record the exact failed lookup and its limits. Saving a public page today, where appropriate, creates a present-day capture. It cannot recover a past version. Respect access restrictions and removal requests.

## Check the artist profile

Read the accepted artist record and its cited claims first. For each of the four coverage notes, identify the evidence used or the remaining question. Check the stage name, role, grime connection and possible aliases against a named appearance or credit. Give the relationship to a city or town in the source’s terms. Use the source records for biographical facts. Describe a documented crew membership, performance, peer recommendation or collaboration precisely and leave the next named lead under its original source. A lone credit may be all that survives. An unknown detail can stay open.

For activity, inspect first-hand interviews, dated release credits, broadcaster archives and contemporary event reports. Use `dated_activity` for a particular, evidenced performance, release or other work. State what happened and when. Keep a recording date, performance date, release date and later upload separate. A flyer can support a scheduled appearance. It takes a further source to establish that the performance happened. Use `career_start`, `career_end` or `active_period` only when the source explicitly establishes a start, end or period. Preserve the source’s precision and any interruption. A first or last surviving trace does not establish a career boundary. Recent activity, silence and a still-live account cannot establish current status. Record inconclusive checks with URLs and findings. For a physical-only item, record the dated fact and locator under the source’s collection reference and do not manufacture a URL.

For booking, start with the artist’s own website or verified profiles. Follow public links labelled for bookings, or open a named representative’s artist-specific booking page. Confirm identity through role, city or a shared credit. Record the public page URL, who provides it, its precise locator and the date checked. Leave the route null for stale directories, generic forms, DMs, direct email or telephone details, or uncertain representation. A professional enquiry form does not establish current availability, fees or an open diary. Never contact the artist or agency, send a booking enquiry, request permission on the contributor’s behalf, or submit an artist-page claim. Ownership and availability are for the artist or an authorised representative to declare. Keep private details out of packets, URLs and public checkpoints.

For short runs, use previously reviewed evidence with its source or revision and prioritise the assigned question. Record what can be checked within the time and name the next lookup for remaining gaps. If a required search could not be made or recovered from a documented check, save an unfinished draft at the deadline. Only a completed check belongs in a search log.

## Find a picture and the artist’s own links

Start with the artist’s website or link page, label, photographer, event archive and their own releases. Follow stage names, old handles and credited collaborators. Check Commons, licence-filtered Flickr and original interviews. A search filter is a lead. Open the item itself and inspect its credits and terms. Save exact pages checked so another person can carry on when time runs out.

Prefer a portrait. Identify the person through the photographer’s caption, an on-screen name or a spoken introduction with a timestamp. For a group shot, identify which person is which. Match the named person to the catalogue through a known credit, crew, performance or artist-owned account. Looking similar, having the same name, or appearing in search results is insufficient.

Check who made the photograph or footage, what the licence covers, and which credit and changes have to appear with it. Trace earlier uses if authorship is unclear. A video’s music, an embedded photograph and the original camera footage can have different owners. If a usable portrait cannot be found, look for artwork for an identified release or an artist-authorised illustration. Verify the artist–work connection and the rights to the **image itself**. A free download or a licence on the recording alone does not clear its cover. Record the title and label it as artwork. If neither option clears both checks, leave the image empty and log the best lead to revisit. Keep permission correspondence private.

Follow the artist or label’s outgoing links to their actual profiles. Check websites, Instagram, YouTube channels, SoundCloud, Bandcamp and other platforms the artist uses. Open each destination, resolve redirects and cross-check a release, collaborator, city or stable channel ID. If a platform requires login, record that limit and look for an independent first-party cross-link. Put confirmed direct profiles in the catalogue. Keep playlists, fan accounts, generic platform homepages, stale handles and unresolved namesakes in the search notes. Lead with the places listeners can hear or follow the artist.

## Review the result

An editor should be able to open the source, find the line and reproduce the exact relationship. An artist’s testimony can establish whom they remember. An uploader’s own credit can establish the role printed on that upload. A mirror, a repost and a Wayback capture of that upload share one origin. Record contrary evidence and preserve uncertainty over aliases, membership and place until it is resolved.

Compare methods by **new people with inspectable evidence**, mistaken merges or locations averted, and whether another person can follow the next lead. Compare quiet scenes and older sources alongside easy-to-search artists. Real Derby trials and outstanding questions are recorded in the [field notes](trials/derby.md).

## Reading an old video credit

Start with the original watch ID when web search turns up only a repost. Open the publisher's video and expand its description. Save the performer billing, producer or camera credit, and the time of any on-screen name. If the page works but playback does not, record what was visible. A title card inspected in a player preview can establish the words on that frame. Describe that inspection in the locator and leave audio marked unchecked. Keep the upload date separate from the recording date.
